How the weights work

31 weak spots, weighted by how much they hurt

Each weak spot has a weight, and the weights add up to 100%. The survey uses them to score your process. I set each weight with three questions:

  • How much money does it put at risk? Direct leaks, such as an overpaid invoice, a missed service credit, or a price increase above the cap, weigh the most.
  • How common is the gap? Weak spots most companies still have weigh more than ones most have already fixed, such as e-signature.
  • Do other fixes depend on it? Integration weighs more because obligation tracking and invoice checks cannot work without it.

The weights are my judgment from field work, not results from a study. My field work is in indirect procurement, so the weights reflect that. If you buy direct materials, you'd probably weigh supplier performance, volume tiers, and rebates higher. Some weak spots tend to fail together. Downstream integration, invoice validation, and the contract-to-spend link often share one root cause, so fixing it can lift all three.

My take on the 31 weak spots

I expected most weak spots to sit inside the contract tool, but the list says otherwise. Many of them are about connections to other systems, not features inside the tool. When I reviewed contracting, sourcing, supplier management, the platform, and procure to pay side by side, the weak spots kept showing up where one system had to talk to the next.

Speed is what the business feels first. A sourcing manager mostly notices that renewals and approvals take longer than they should, and starts working around the system. Slow cycles are real, but they are not where most of the money goes, so they carry less weight here.

The weak spots after signature cost more. Savings leakage and off-contract spend only became clear to me when I followed the money. Missed renewals alone cost a few million dollars a year. Those weak spots are quiet, so they tend to get fixed last.

Where value leaks

The handoffs

The contract itself is rarely the weak point. The breaks sit where one system or team hands work to the next.

My take on the handoffs

Most of the pain I saw started after signature. A contract term had to reach the purchase order and the invoice, and often nothing carried it there. That is why I think about handoffs first and features second.

Payment terms are a good example. The contract said one thing, the payables system used the supplier's default term, and the default won. Multi-year purchase orders caused a quieter version of the same problem. Buyers added a new line with the same description each year, and invoice reading could not pick the right line. The best way to fix that is to tighten what you ask of suppliers on their invoices. That fix does not need a new contract tool.

Postings from companies buying this kind of technology keep asking for touchless procure to pay and automation, so buyers feel the same break. Touchless only works if contract terms reach the transaction. When I judge a tool now, I look at how well it hands work off, and I want a named owner for each handoff.

Intake and sourcing to the contract
Request, supplier, and award data from intake, sourcing, and RFx do not flow into the contract, so buyers re-enter it when drafting.
The contract to the PO and invoice
Contract pricing and terms do not carry into requisitions and POs. POs are created by hand and often do not match the contract.
The invoice back to the contract
Invoice prices, discounts, and payment terms are not checked against the contract at the line level. The result is overpayment and unenforced terms.
The contract to the ERP
The contract does not use the ERP's spend categories, cost centers, locations, or accounting fields, and it cannot see the approval hierarchy or owners who have left.
The renewal notice to the renewal work
The system sends a renewal notice, but the rest of the renewal process happens outside it.

My take on the weak spots, one by one

Read one at a time, most of these weak spots trace back to data that a person had to type or move by hand. People entered contract data, and they read the renewal field in different ways. Search was slow, trust in the data dropped, and then use dropped with it.

If I had to fix weak spots in order, I would start with the ones that cost the most money, such as renewals and price increases. In one case a supplier asked for a 10% increase on a contract capped at 2.5%, and we caught it because we could search by clause. A missed renewal can cost a few million dollars a year. A weak filter costs far less.

The adoption and trust weak spots look soft, but they matter. Sourcing managers moved to general AI tools because those tools let them combine documents and project notes in one place. When people leave your system because it cannot support the level of analysis they need to make decisions, the data in your system gets worse, so I count trust and adoption alongside the technical weak spots.

What closes the weak spots

How the solutions fit together

The potential solutions in the analysis fall into four groups, and the order matters.

My take on the solutions

The order of the solutions matters. I would connect the chain first, because automation on top of disconnected systems only delivers the wrong answer faster. Integration is less exciting than AI, but everything else depends on it.

Agents are the part I find most interesting. Renewals and negotiation prep are repeat work with the same steps each time. An agent that pulls vendor performance and contract history together, with market data, before a sourcing manager sits down can change how that person spends the week. It only earns trust if every answer shows its source, so a person can check it in seconds.

The bigger shift is how companies see contract management. Postings in this space describe moving it from administrative overhead to a strategic advantage, through better technology and data visibility. I agree with that direction. The proof a CFO accepts is dollars, such as renewals caught and price increases stopped.

Connect the chain

Integrations from intake and sourcing through requisition, PO, invoice, and the ERP, so contract terms follow the transaction.

Automate the follow-up work

Agents that run renewals, prepare negotiation material, track obligations and SLAs, and start a claim when a trigger is missed.

Show the source

Citations back to the clause, confidence scores, and human review, so people trust the answer and stop re-checking by hand.

Put procurement in control

No-code configuration, an easy procurement front door, and dashboards by persona, so the business uses the system.

Bonus

For the people who build these tools

Contract management is a crowded market. From someone who sat on the buyer's side, here is where a vendor can stand apart.

Sell the money after signature.

Most vendors pitch faster drafting and redlining. A CFO funds what gets captured after the contract is signed: renewals caught, price increases stopped, payment terms enforced. A supplier asking for a 10% increase on a contract capped at 2.5% is the kind of proof that wins budget.

Build for procurement, not only legal.

Many contract tools started with legal. Procurement needs the terms to reach the purchase order, the invoice, and the ERP. That handoff is where most of the weak spots on this page sit, and few vendors own it well.

Earn trust in the AI, and show value fast.

Every vendor claims AI now. What sets one apart is answers that point to the exact clause, and setup that procurement can change without a long IT project. When people don't trust the answers, they move to general AI tools, and the data in the system gets worse.
